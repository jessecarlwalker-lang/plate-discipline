The Game That Rewired October

One man. One night. Every conceivable limit broken at once.

Dodgers 5, Brewers 1 · NLCS Game 4
The Setup

Shohei Ohtani was slumping. Coming into Game 4 of the NLCS, he'd managed just 3 hits in 29 at-bats over his previous 7 games (a .103 average with 1 extra-base hit). The Brewers had knotted the series at 2-2. The World Series hung in the balance. This was the moment where postseason narratives either shift or collapse.

What happened next breaks baseball's usual categories. A single athlete performing at a level so complete that opposing hitters watched the ball travel 1,342 feet across the diamond while being unable to touch it, then returned to the bench to face a pitcher throwing 100 pitches they couldn't hit either.

Dave Roberts would call it "probably the greatest postseason performance of all time." He's managed a lot of postseasons. He wasn't exaggerating.

The Hitting

Three Home Runs. Perfect Swings.

Ohtani finished 3-for-3 with 3 solo home runs. You can recite that sentence and still not understand what happened. He hit the 3 hardest-hit balls of the night. He hit the 3 farthest balls of the night. Perfect contact, three times.

The first home run came off Jose Quintana in the 1st inning. 446 feet. 115.6 mph exit velo. Then in the 4th, another one into a different part of the park: 469 feet. In the 7th, Ohtani lined one out. 113.6 mph, a liner, not even requiring the conventional arc. The Brewers were watching a man hit a different home run each time he stepped to the plate.

He was the 12th player in postseason history to hit 3 home runs in a single game. He was the first to do it while simultaneously pitching 6 shutout innings. The second statistic matters more than the first.

The Pitching

Ten Strikeouts. Zero Runs. Zero Chance.

After hitting 3 home runs, Ohtani took the mound in the 2nd inning. The Brewers batters faced something they'd never encountered: a hitter's energy converted directly into pitching performance. He threw 100 pitches (the most since his Tommy John return). He allowed 2 hits. He struck out 10. His ERA for the night: infinity divided by zero, which in baseball terms is just called "untouchable."

The splitter was the story. Brewers hitters missed all 5 swings at it. Five swings. Five whiffs. The pitch that normally generates weak contact instead generated nothing; the batter's bat slicing through empty air. Across the entire postseason, the splitter generated only 19 total swings-and-misses. Ohtani threw it 5 times and got 5.

This was the pennant-clinching game. Ohtani pitched 6.0 innings with 10 strikeouts and 0 earned runs. The only pitcher in postseason history to do this in a pennant-clinching game.

The Context

Breaking Every Frame

What He Was
Coming in: 3-for-29 (.103) in his last 7 games. 1 XBH. A slump that looked less like a slump and more like a cliff. The postseason narrative had already started shifting. Would the Dodgers survive his absence?
What He Became
NLCS MVP. The slump evaporated in 3 swings. He was the hardest-hitting pitcher, led all pitchers in swings-and-misses, threw the 11 hardest pitches of the night, and did it all in a pennant-clinching performance.

The Dodgers rotation had posted a 0.63 ERA with 35 strikeouts in 28.2 innings during the NLCS. That stat is already extraordinary. Ohtani's single game made it seem almost normal by comparison.

"Probably the greatest postseason performance of all time."
— Dave Roberts, Dodgers Manager

Roberts has managed through October more than almost anyone. His words carry weight because he's seen everything. This was beyond everything.

The Meaning

One Game Rewired Expectations

Baseball has a strict taxonomy for postseason performances. There are great games, historic games, legendary games. Ohtani's October 17 performance required a new category. Every skill set, deployed simultaneously, refusing to let any single human limitation apply.

After hitting 3 home runs, he took the ball and threw 100 pitches. His splitter worked so well that hitters stopped swinging at it. His fastball was so hard that hitters couldn't catch up. Simultaneously, he'd already provided all the offense his team needed.

A single player ended the series by performing at a level that made the opposition seem like they were playing a different sport. The Dodgers advanced to the World Series. Ohtani had ensured it wouldn't be close. He'd turned a pressure moment into a statement.

That's what the greatest postseason performance of all time looks like: one man, one night, every single skill deployed at maximum capacity. The result was inevitable. The method was unforgettable.
